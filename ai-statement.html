Statement on AI and Scientific Research

How this lab uses, and declines to use, AI tools in scientific work. It applies to our research, to what we publish, and to the code and data we release.

01 Principles

The people in this lab believe in the following principles

  • Tools amplify, they do not replace

    In the hands of someone who already understands the problem, a tool is a force multiplier. It sharpens and speeds up work, but it cannot substitute for the knowledge required to use it well. For the same reason, a tool used without responsible direction can just as readily amplify bias, discrimination, and harm.

  • Tools do not make decisions

    However capable a tool is, the judgment calls remain ours. A tool can inform a decision; it cannot stand in for the process of making one.

  • Some tedious work is part of the process, not an obstacle to it

    Even where a tool could reliably automate a manual task, we do not concede that step by default. Certain work teaches us something precisely because it is slow and manual, and we protect the right to keep doing it by hand.

02 Statement

On the use of AI in our scientific work

Based on these principles, and on the work and thoughts of other researchers [C], we have developed the following statement.

“AI” refers to a diverse set of automated and semiautomated technologies including (but not limited to) predictive machine learning systems, automated transcription, generative models that produce images and text, chatbots built on large language models (LLMs), and coding agents that automate programming tasks. Some forms of this technology offer substantial benefits for our research, while other forms threaten our core values. [C]

The lab holds two priorities as equal: producing reliable knowledge and supporting the development of knowers, namely students, teachers, researchers, and practitioners. Neither one comes first. There is no knowledge without knowers who exist in community with one another, and we are committed to sustaining that community so the practice of science can continue.

Papers and reports document our work so that others can learn from it and build on it. Software and data give that work a working, reproducible form, so others can run it, check it, and extend it. Neither is the goal. The goal is to improve our understanding of the world, to build our knowledge and skills as scientists, and to pass that knowledge and those skills on, so that the world benefits from them. Reliability and trust are the guiding principle behind both priorities: we need knowledge that can be trusted, and we need the knowers who produce it to be trustworthy in turn. We are concerned that the widespread and uncritical use of AI tools in science erodes the personal relationships and collective learning on which both priorities depend.

We are also concerned about the widespread use of proprietary models for producing scientific findings, which threatens transparency and reproducibility, cornerstones of robust science. As a rule, we do not rely on the conclusions of proprietary models as findings in their own right; any result we report must be reproducible on inspectable, fixed machinery, not on an opaque system whose workings we cannot inspect or rerun identically.

We recognize that many of the companies developing AI tools engage in unethical business practices, e.g., by training their models on stolen data and exploiting the labor of vulnerable people. We are alarmed by industrial development of power-hungry data centers during a climate emergency, and by the deployment of chatbot products that harm mental health. When we use AI tools, particularly those developed by profit-seeking companies, we are complicit in these harms. Open, local models already exist, and we expect them to become practical to run alongside proprietary ones. We do not build new models ourselves; our research is aimed at building applications that work on top of these open alternatives, so that dependence on proprietary products is a choice, not a necessity.

These values and acknowledgments inform our approach to incorporating AI in our scientific work. We agree with the position taken elsewhere [C] that many of the potential benefits and threats of this technology cannot be summarized in a generic lab policy; rather, the tradeoffs depend on the specifics of the project, the AI tool in question, and the training stage of the lab member.

Every lab member develops a personal AI policy in consultation with their mentors, revisited each semester. We support conscientious refusal as a valid choice, and collaborative projects must respect and communicate each member’s policy from the outset.

03 Guidelines

Example guidelines

How we currently handle the situations that come up most often. They are worked examples for a personal policy to start from, not a rule that settles every case.

Peer review
AI tools are never used to review a manuscript or grant. They may be used to reformulate an already-written review for language and clarity, provided no private or de-anonymizing information is shared in the process.
Presentations
Use of AI to draft a presentation is strongly discouraged. Building a talk is a manual process because it is a dialogue with the material, and it requires the presenter to think deeply about what is being presented. Delegating that drafting is delegating the thinking itself. AI tools may still be used to template, summarize, or translate a message that has already been written by the presenter.
Coding
AI tools may be used to translate precise, already-specified requirements into code. They are never used to decide on technology choices, nor to invent algorithms; both require thinking and a decision made beforehand by the researcher, in part because we have no clear way to attribute authorship for an algorithm we did not design. Tests are always written first. Using AI to search for bugs is acceptable, provided we acknowledge that such a search is not exhaustive and its findings are double-checked.
Diagrams
AI tools may be used to translate information into an editable diagram, but only once that information is already understood and described by the person making the diagram. Making a diagram is itself part of understanding, and the result must remain manually editable. We never produce diagrams that cannot be edited by hand, since a fixed, non-editable diagram discourages the manual fixing that is part of getting it right.
Finding citations
AI tools may be used to help locate candidate citations, but every citation used in our work is read and assessed by the author before it is included. We verify, for each one, that the paper exists, that it is relevant to the claim it supports, that it actually contains the content being claimed, and that all bibliographic data is correct.

We will discuss and revisit this statement annually and revise it in light of our experiences and new information.

How this document was written

This document itself was produced with the help of an AI tool, used to reformulate and restructure the original Crockett Lab statement into this one. The process was directed sentence by sentence by the editor (Matteo Lissandrini): for each passage, the editor specified what needed to change and why, the AI tool proposed alternative wordings, and the lab selected, edited, or rejected each proposal before it was included. The editor also reviewed the final text to ensure it met the lab’s standards and requirements.

Every sentence carried over unchanged from the original is marked [C]; a marker is removed once its paragraph has been reformulated through this process.
